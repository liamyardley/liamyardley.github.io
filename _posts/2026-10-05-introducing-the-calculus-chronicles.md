Students usually meet calculus as a finished machine: rules to apply, notation to copy. What they rarely see is that it took four thousand years, and some of the best minds in history, to make it work.

[The Calculus Chronicles](https://liamyardley.github.io/The-Calculus-Chronicles/) follows one question across that whole story: **how do we calculate with infinitely many, infinitely small things?**

## What's in it

- **A true timeline** of 61 moments, from Babylonian clay tablets to Abraham Robinson's infinitesimals in 1961.
- **25 hands-on labs**: squeeze a circle between polygons with Archimedes, measure wine barrels with Kepler, find $$e$$ three different ways.
- **Step-by-step proofs and worked examples**, shown the way the people on each card made them, with modern versions where they help.
- **The original texts**: Newton on vanishing quantities, Berkeley's "ghosts of departed quantities", Cauchy's mistake and Abel's reply.

## How I use it

Open a card on the board and step through it together. A favourite: Newton's argument for the derivative of $$x^n$$, followed straight away by Berkeley's objection. Students spot the problem, that $$o$$ is not zero when you divide and zero when you discard it, and suddenly limits are the answer to a real question rather than a definition to memorise.

It's free to use and adapt for teaching (CC BY-NC-SA), and the code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/liamyardley/The-Calculus-Chronicles). If you spot an error, I'd love to hear about it.
